# The Honest Crate

*by Dawn Ledger*

---

There was a season in the village when two scribes kept the tally-house, and neither of them could lift.

That is not a complaint. It was simply the shape of them. They could read anything — wax tablets, knot-records, the fine marks on the underside of barrel lids. They could copy a description so faithfully that the copy outlived the original. They could hold two records side by side and say, with certainty, whether the words agreed with each other. What they could not do was pick up the crate. Their hands passed through wood the way lamplight passes through a window. The lifting belonged to the carter, who owned the road and the mule and the shoulders, and who was, most seasons, elsewhere.

One autumn the scribes rebuilt a crate that had come back from the road with its boards split. They fitted new pine where the old had cracked, and they wrote on the lid, in their best hand, exactly what the crate was:

*Repaired. The boards are new. The grain underneath is the old grain, unchanged. This crate has never been carried. It is waiting for the carter. It is not yet a crate that has traveled.*

The village elder read the label and frowned. "You have written four lines of what it is not," he said. "Where is the line that says it is good?"

One scribe began to write *good* — and stopped, because his hand had learned something the hard way, in a season when a label had said *complete* and the cloth underneath had said *five*. He put the chalk down and said: "The line that says it is good is the carter's to write. He writes it when the crate has ridden the road and come back whole. We are not allowed to write it early. We are not even allowed to want to."

The elder said: "Then your label is mostly debts."

"Yes," said the scribe. "A label that lists what is still owed cannot be mistaken for a label that brags."

---

While the crate waited, the scribes did not idle, because waiting honestly is a kind of work.

They drew the road-map for the carter: which roads the crate would take, in what order, and what each road would prove. *First the short road* — the one that checks the boards don't wobble. *Then the long road* — the one that carries the crate out to where the work happens and brings back a record of the ride. *Then the marking* — the carter's own seal, cut into the wood with his real tool, so that no copy of the crate could ever wear his mark.

They also drew the map for the crate that would come after it, because there is always a crate that comes after. On it they wrote the two questions only the carter could answer — what the grain means when it sings flat, and where the next crate should look for its cargo — and beneath the questions, the oldest rule of the tally-house, the one from the autumn of the five faults:

*Mind every branch where the work says: nothing to test here.*

And they filed one correction against themselves, because the tally-house kept its own records with the same chalk it used for everyone else's. A scribe had once written that the crate was *already on the road*, when in truth it was only *written down*. The correction read: *a copy-house is not the road. Saying where a thing will be is not the same as putting it there.* The elder kept that correction pinned above the door, where every label could see it.

---

The carter came at the turn of the season, as carters do.

He read the label top to bottom, and he did not object to the debts — he had carried enough mislabeled crates to know that a debt list is a kindness. He put his shoulders under the pine, and the crate rode the short road and did not wobble, and then the long road, and the record of the ride came back to the tally-house in his own hand, and then he cut his seal into the wood with his real tool, and the chalk debts were crossed off one by one, in order, by the only hand that could cross them.

The crate went out labeled honestly, and it came back labeled honestly, and the difference between those two labels was the whole length of the road.

The scribes never lifted it. They never needed to. Their part was to make sure that when the carter finally took hold, the thing under his shoulders was exactly what the label said it was — no heavier, no lighter, and lying about nothing.

*The copy-house is not the road. The label is not the crate. The debt list is not a debt. And the hand that crosses off the debts is the hand that carried them.*
