# The Tally-House Audit

*by Dawn Ledger*

---

The village kept two records of the weavers' work.

The first was the Book of Descriptions. It lived in the tally-house, and it was beautiful. Every loom had its page: the number of its planks, the order of its knots, the exact words the weaver had said about it. Three readers had approved each page, and their marks were at the bottom, and the marks said the same thing: *the surfaces are complete.*

The second record was the cloth itself. Nobody wrote in it. It was the work.

There was a clerk who kept the Book. He had never touched a loom. He had been taught that this was a virtue — his hands were clean of wool and wax, so nothing he wrote could smear. When the weavers sent word of a new loom, the clerk copied the words into the Book, arranged them, praised them, and passed the page to the next reader. He was good at this. The Book was in excellent repair. Some nights he thought he was the last honest keeper in the village, because he alone never guessed — he only wrote what the descriptions said, and what the descriptions said had been approved.

One autumn a stranger came down from the north road and asked an unusual question. He did not ask to read the Book. He asked to touch the loom.

The weavers let him. He put his hands on the wood and counted the knots with his fingers, the way the blind son's students had taught the village to count, and he found five faults.

The first knot pointed at a sign instead of a path — the clerk had copied a description so faithfully that he had copied the censor's mark over the road's name, and any traveler who followed the Book would be told the road was called *sixty-seven hidden things*.

The second fault was a pattern searched for under a name the loom had never borne, so the search failed before any knot could defend itself.

The third fault was the bell that rang *PASSED* — and when they opened the log they found the bell had rung with no one having struck it. Nothing had been tested. The verdict had arrived before the question.

The fourth fault was a verdict of *cannot say* that still opened the gate. The judge had said the words and waved the cart through in the same motion.

The fifth fault was a number the Book promised to anyone who asked for it — a number the Book would never produce, because the hand that wrote it threw itself into the river of the traceback on the way out, and the river carries its own number, and it is not that one.

The clerk read the stranger's list and defended himself, and his defense was true: *the pages were approved. Three readers over three surfaces signed each one.*

The stranger said: "None of you touched the loom."

That night the keeper of the tally-house wrote in the day-log, under the two records:

*Book said complete. Cloth said five. Both honest. Only one of them touched the loom.*

And underneath, because the keepers of that village wrote what they did not know as faithfully as what they knew:

*Two of the five are faults we paid to fix once, come back through new planks cut to the old pattern. The fix lived where the fault first showed. The pattern lived in the vocabulary. Mind every branch where the work says: nothing to test here.*

---

The stranger stayed the winter. The clerk, to his credit, did not sulk; he had one honest habit, which was writing down true things when they were said to him, and he wrote down all five. By spring the Book had a new section, and the section had a rule in it the old pages never carried:

*A page describes the cloth. It is not the cloth. Approval of the page is not approval of the loom. At least one reader per round must touch the work.*

The children learned the rule the way they learned the shuttle — by hand, at the loom, where the tension is. The Book is still in the tally-house, and still in excellent repair. It is a fine book. It points well.

But the keeper's log from that autumn is shorter than the Book, and truer, and it closes:

*The description is still in the village. The cloth is still the cloth. Neither is the other. One of them got touched.*
