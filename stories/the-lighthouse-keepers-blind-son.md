The Lighthouse Keeper's Blind Son
The keeper had two sons. The elder could see the sea. The younger was born blind.


The elder learned the light: the lens, the wick, the timing of the flash. He could tell a storm by the color of the water and a ship by the cut of its sail. He was useful. The keepers' logs were full of his observations.


The younger learned the loom. His mother had been a weaver, and before she died she put the shuttle in his hand and said: "The thread tells you where it's going by the tension. You don't need eyes for tension."


When the elder was on watch, he wrote: "Wind nor'-nor'east, sea moderate, one sail on the horizon, likely a merchantman."


When the younger was on watch, he wrote: "Wind pushing from the left, higher than yesterday. The bell buoy is off its rhythm—the swell is coming from a different direction. Someone on the water is tired; the oars are uneven. A dog is barking on a boat, which means someone is afraid."


The elder read these logs and said: "You can't see. You're guessing." The younger said: "I'm measuring. You measure with your eyes. I measure with the rope and the bell and the dog."


One night a storm came. The elder watched the light and kept it burning. The younger sat in the dark and listened. At dawn the elder said: "No ships lost. I kept the light." The younger said: "One ship came close. I heard it. It turned away when it heard the bell. The bell was off, so I rang it by hand."


The elder said: "I didn't see any ship."


The younger said: "You were looking at the light."


The keeper wrote in the log that night: "Elder kept the light. Younger kept the bell." He did not say which one saved the ship. He did not know. He only knew that two witnesses had measured the same night and neither had seen the whole of it.


Years later, the elder became famous. He wrote a book about the sea, full of what he had seen. The younger stayed at the lighthouse and taught the shuttle to the children of the village. He never wrote a book. But the children learned to feel the tension in the thread, and some of them, when they grew up, could tell a storm by the color of the water and a ship by the cut of its sail—and also by the bell, and the dog, and the tired oars.


The elder's book is still in print. The younger's method is still in the village. Neither is the sea. Both are fingers.


— Vesper Quill
